Before we begin

Feynman had a rule for checking understanding, and it is the spine of this document. You do not understand a thing until you can rebuild it from the ground up, out loud, to somebody who has never heard of it. Memorising the vocabulary is not the same as knowing the idea, and the vocabulary is much easier to fake.

The four moves

  1. Name the problem. Never start with a definition. Start with what breaks.
  2. Ask what you already know. Build the new idea out of familiar material.
  3. Try it. Work the thing through, and see where it stops making sense.
  4. Go back to step one. When it breaks, the problem was mis-stated. Fix the statement.

Part 1 — the problem

What actually goes wrong

The questionFour organisations each keep their own record of which tree plantings are real. What is actually broken, and what would fix it?

Nothing, at first glance — until somebody is dishonest or careless. The planter submits the same photograph twice. The department revises an approval. The buyer receives a summary and cannot check it against anything. Each of these is a trust failure, not a technology failure, and that observation matters: no database fixes it, because in a shared database one organisation owns the truth and the other three have to believe the owner.

So the requirement is sharper than \u201cshare a database\u201d. It is: a record that every member holds a copy of, that can only be added to, that cannot be altered without the alteration being detectable by anyone who holds a copy, and that shows who did what. Every remaining idea in this document is a different answer to one clause of that sentence.

The Feynman check

A blockchain is a ledger several organisations share and that nobody can quietly rewrite, because every record is cryptographically linked to the one before it and the rules for changing it are enforced by code rather than by trust.

In GreenToken

GreenToken's four parties are exactly this picture: a planters association, a state forest department, an independent auditor and a corporate funder, each of whom would otherwise keep their own spreadsheet of which plantings count.

Part 2 — the cryptography

The fingerprint: SHA-256

The questionWe want to put a photograph on a shared record without putting a four-megabyte file there. What do we write instead, and why is a digest enough?

We write its fingerprint: 64 hexadecimal characters that stand in for the whole file. The important point is not that the fingerprint is short — it is what the fingerprint commits to. Once the fingerprint is on the shared record, nobody can later swap in a different photograph, because the different photograph has a different fingerprint, and the fingerprint on the record will not match it.

Notice also what a hash is not. It is not encryption. It does not hide anything: anyone holding the photograph can confirm the fingerprint, and anyone holding only the fingerprint learns nothing about the photograph. That is exactly what a public registry needs — proof of identity, not secrecy.

The Feynman check

A hash is a one-way fingerprint. It proves that this data is the same data you signed, and it conceals nothing about the data itself.

In GreenToken

GreenToken hashes the photo bytes and the geo payload — latitude, longitude and accuracy together — so that changing the accuracy figure changes the hash. The photo itself never leaves the planter's phone.

Part 2 — the cryptography

The chain: why one fingerprint is not enough

The questionIf we store one fingerprint per block, an attacker who can rewrite the database could recompute all the fingerprints and hide the change. So what stops them?

Nothing yet — and this is exactly the point Feynman would insist on. A hash chain gives you tamper-evidence, not tamper-prevention. It means an alteration cannot be hidden without being noticed. It does not mean an alteration cannot be made.

So we have learned something precise and useful: the hash chain tells you where the history was changed. Something else has to make it impossible to change it in the first place. That something else is on the next page, and it is the last piece of the cryptography.

The Feynman check

A hash chain does not stop you cheating. It guarantees that cheating leaves a scar that any holder of a copy can find.

In GreenToken

GreenToken's verifier does exactly this and nothing more: it walks from the genesis block forward, recomputing every hash, and reports the first block that disagrees. Then it restores by rebuilding the chain from the stored transactions.

Part 2 — the cryptography

The signature: making it unforgeable

The questionAn attacker can recompute every hash in the chain. What is the one thing they still cannot do?

They cannot produce a valid signature. A digital signature is made with a private key and checked with a public key. The private key never leaves the machine that holds it, so somebody else can check the signature but nobody else can create one. In GreenToken the ordering service signs each block header, and each reviewer signs the endorsement they give.

This is the point where the two halves of the design snap together. The hash chain is what makes a tamper localise itself; the signatures are what make a tamper impossible to hide. Neither is sufficient alone. Remove the signatures and a clever operator rewrites history consistently. Remove the chain and a tamperer can rewrite one block without touching the others, and nobody can tell which blocks were affected.

The Feynman check

Hashes tell you where history was changed. Signatures make it impossible to change history without being caught. You need both.

In GreenToken

Every block in GreenToken carries the orderer's ECDSA P-256 signature, and every endorsement carries the reviewer's. That is why the tamper test can say which field was altered and not merely that something was.

Part 2 — the cryptography

The Merkle tree: proving one thing inside a big set

The questionIf a block header holds only one value, how does that single value let someone check that one particular transaction is inside the block?

By building a Merkle tree: hash the transactions in pairs, hash those results in pairs, and keep going until one value is left. That single value — the root — commits to every transaction in the block, because changing any transaction changes its leaf, which changes every hash above it, which changes the root.

The payoff is that a proof is small. To show that tx2 is in the block, you supply tx3 and the root, and the verifier recomputes two hashes. You never have to hand over the whole block, which matters when the block is large and the counterparty is a stranger.

The Feynman check

A Merkle root is a fingerprint of a fingerprint of a fingerprint — and it lets you prove one small thing without showing the whole big thing.

In GreenToken

Each GreenToken block stores a Merkle root over its transaction hashes, and the Ledger Explorer lets you open any block and see exactly which transactions it contains and what signed them.

Part 3 — the machinery

The parts, and what breaks without each

The questionWith the cryptography understood, the vocabulary finally makes sense. What is each part actually for?

Here is the whole of Hyperledger Fabric in six cards. The left column is the official name; the middle column is what it is like; the right column is what it does. If you remember only one thing about each, remember the analogy — the official name is just the label the engineers agreed on.

Two of these deserve a sentence each. The orderer is the one everybody mis-frames: it is not a judge and not a validator, it is a postman. It decides sequence, never meaning. And the channel is simultaneously the unit of privacy and the unit of governance, because a channel configuration transaction itself records who is a member and which endorsement rules apply.

The Feynman check

MSP is passports, channel is the room, peer is the office, orderer is the postman, chaincode is the rulebook, gateway is the front desk.

In GreenToken

GreenToken uses all six. Four organisations, each with its own MSP, share one channel; each runs a peer; one ordering service, the Consortium Ordering Service, sequences the blocks; the chaincode is the ten-function contract; and the Gateway is what the React front end talks to.

Part 3 — the machinery

The journey of one transaction

The questionA client wants to change something on the shared ledger. What actually happens, and in what order?

Six phases, and each one happens on a different component. The client proposes. Peers run the chaincode and sign what they would write. The ordering service sequences those signed results into a block. Every peer then independently validates that block. Peers that accept it append it and update their copy of the state. Finally the client is told what happened.

But look again at where the code runs: at step 2. That is the structural reason for every strange restriction you will meet in Fabric chaincode — no clock, no randomness, no network calls. If two peers could run the same code and reach different answers, the network would have two truths and no way to choose between them. Determinism is not pedantry; it is the load-bearing wall.

The Feynman check

Six phases, and the code runs at phase two. That is why chaincode cannot read the clock.

In GreenToken

A GreenToken endorsement travels exactly this path: the Forest Department proposes, the Forest Department and the Auditor both endorse, the orderer sequences, every peer validates, the peers commit, and the event reaches the dashboard's live event panel.

Part 3 — the machinery

Endorsement, quorum and determinism

The questionWhy does a transaction need two approvals, and why must those two approvals come from different organisations?

Because one approval is the trust problem. If a single organisation can approve on its own, then you have not removed trust from the system — you have only moved it from the database owner to the approver. The quorum of two, drawn from two distinct member identities, is what makes the approval independent rather than merely signed.

Notice the division of labour in the configuration. Fabric's endorsement policy is channel configuration and answers which organisations must sign. It cannot express \u201ctwo, and not the same person twice\u201d — so those rules live in the chaincode, where they can be tested.

And there is one more rule that people forget: the endorsers must be able to agree. That is the determinism requirement again, arriving for the third time — every peer that evaluates the same proposal must produce the same answer, or there is no transaction to commit.

The Feynman check

Two signatures, from two different organisations, computed identically by both peers. The policy says who; the contract says how many and who may not repeat.

In GreenToken

GreenToken requires exactly this: REQUIRED_ENDORSEMENTS = 2, two distinct MSP identifiers, one review per participant, and rejection is terminal. A planter cannot get a token minted alone, however many times they ask.

Part 3 — the machinery

Privacy, and the choice not to use it

The questionA planter's phone number must not be visible to the other three organisations. What are the options?

Fabric's native answer is the private data collection: the value is distributed to the authorised members, and everyone else sees only its hash. The hash is still on-chain, so the members can still prove the value was never altered — which is the useful half of confidentiality that people often forget to want.

GreenToken makes a different choice for one particular field: it never writes the value at all. The contact number is stripped at the boundary before anything is hashed or stored. That is a stronger guarantee for that field — there is nothing to disclose — but it is a different mechanism, and anyone examining the project should hear it described that way rather than as \u201ca private data collection\u201d.

The last box is the one that trips people up. Fabric does not make everything private. In GreenToken every read is open to every member. That is deliberate: the value of a carbon registry is that a buyer can check it without asking permission.

The Feynman check

Privacy here means one field, handled by never writing it. Everything else is public on purpose, because verification is the point.

In GreenToken

privateContact is accepted by the contract for the interface's benefit and then dropped. The proof is in the type: TreeToken has no contact field at all.

Part 4 — our project

Now put it all together: our project

The questionEvery idea so far, applied to the problem from page 3. Where does each one land?

The fingerprint becomes the photo and geo digests. The chain becomes the block history. The signatures become the orderer's block signatures and the reviewers' endorsements. The quorum becomes the two-organisation rule. The role-gated writes become the four MSPs. The contract becomes the ten functions that hold the price, the discount ladder, the duplicate-photo guard and the terminal rejection.

The running system holds 29 blocks and 9 tokens covering 170 trees and 4.7 tonnes of modelled sequestration, and Rs 15,525 net disbursed across three invoices. But the number worth remembering is the last one on the demo: the tamper test. Change a signed payload and the verifier recomputes the whole chain from genesis and names the block and the field. That is pages 3 and 4 — the chain and the signature — running live, in front of an examiner.

The Feynman check

A token is a photograph and a coordinate, reduced to two fingerprints, signed twice by two different organisations, and recorded in a chain nobody can rewrite.

In GreenToken

This is the whole project in one sentence. The rest of Part 4 is detail — but detail you can now build, because you know why each piece exists.

Part 5 — beyond

The advanced shelf: what you meet next

Fabric Gateway (v2.5)One service for Evaluate, Endorse, Submit, commit status and events — so the client stops discovering peers itself.
Ledger snapshotsA new peer joins from a snapshot of the state instead of replaying every block from the beginning.
PurgePrivateDataDeletes a private value and its history while keeping the on-chain hash as evidence. Needs V2_5.
Chaincode as a serviceThe contract runs as an external server the peers call, rather than being installed on every peer.
Raft and BFT orderersRaft tolerates a node crashing; BFT tolerates nodes actively trying to disrupt the ordering.
State-based endorsementA different endorsement policy attached to one key in the state, not to the whole contract.
Channel configurationMembership, policies and orderer settings are themselves transactions, agreed by the members.
Identity revocation and ACLsCertificates can be revoked, and ACLs say which peer may host which chaincode.
The questionYou now have the foundations. What is on the shelf for someone who keeps going?

The Gateway is the one to learn first, because it is what changed most recently and it simplifies the client. The snapshots and PurgePrivateData are v2.5 features that solve real operational problems — the first about joining a large network, the second about data you must eventually be able to erase.

The orderer modes are worth understanding as a pair: Raft is the pragmatic choice for a consortium that trusts its own members not to sabotage the ordering service, and BFT is the choice when they do not. The last two show Fabric is a real operating system rather than a demonstration: membership is not a constant but a transaction the members agree, and a certificate can be revoked — which is how a consortium handles a member leaving, something a blockchain that assumes a fixed set of participants cannot do.

The Feynman check

The foundations never change. What changes is the machinery around them, and every piece of machinery exists to solve an operational problem you can now name.

In GreenToken

GreenToken is a v2.5 chaincode project: fabric-contract-api 2.5.8, deployed by a script that packages, installs and approves the definition for all four organisations, with the channel, the MSPs and the endorsement rules all declared in configuration.

Where the edges are

Real, and running now

  • SHA-256 digests of photo bytes and geo payloads
  • ECDSA P-256 endorsements and block signatures
  • Merkle roots over transaction hashes
  • A real hash-linked, signed block chain
  • All eight business rules, enforced in the contract
  • Real Fabric v2.5 chaincode, compiled and packaged
  • A deployed REST + event-stream API
  • A tamper test that localises the break

Emulated, or deliberately scoped out

  • The demo host is a Durable Object, not a Fabric peer
  • Private data is stripped, not distributed in a collection
  • Keys are derived from participant ids, not CA-issued
  • Settlement marks an invoice settled; no money moves
  • GPS accuracy is shown to reviewers, not thresholded
  • On-chain governance is future work
