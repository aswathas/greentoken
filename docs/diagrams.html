Fabric in one picture

What you are looking at. A client never writes to the ledger itself. It talks to a Gateway — a peer-side service added in Fabric v2.4 and standardised in v2.5 — which owns the five verbs: Evaluate, Endorse, Submit, commit-status and chaincode events. The ordering service only sequences; it never runs chaincode and never checks business rules, because correctness comes from the endorsement and validation phases instead. Each MSP is one organisation's identity directory. The channel is the unit of both privacy and governance — GreenToken's single channel holds all four organisations, and a fifth would not see the ledger at all.
Say it“Five moving parts. The client talks to a Gateway, the Gateway asks peers to endorse, the orderer sequences what they endorsed, every peer validates and commits, and an MSP decides who is allowed in. And the orderer never checks a business rule.”

The consortium — four organisations, one channel

What you are looking at. Every column is one MSP — one organisation's identity directory, with its certificates, its participants and the roles they may play. The rules are asymmetric on purpose: the planters may only plant; the Forest Department and the Auditor may endorse; the funders may only buy; and settlement is granted to the funders and the Auditor, because an independent auditor settling a trade-finance invoice is exactly how a real consortium would work. All four columns hang from the single ordering service and join one channel.
Say it“Four MSPs, one channel, fourteen participants. Each organisation can only do what its role allows — and those permissions are written into the chaincode, not into the user interface. Note that the Auditor can settle invoices as well as endorse them, because settlement needs an independent party.”

The six-phase transaction lifecycle

What you are looking at. These six phases are not optional — every transaction that has ever run on Fabric went through them, and each one happens on a different component. The most common misconception is that the contract runs at commit. It does not: the chaincode executes at endorsement, and by commit time the peers are only checking that the right organisations agreed and that nothing changed underneath. That is exactly why Fabric chaincode must be deterministic.
Say it“Six phases. The one that matters is phase two — the contract runs at endorsement, not at commit. At commit the peers are just validating that two different organisations signed the same result. And because the chaincode already ran, it has to be deterministic: no clock, no randomness, no network calls.”

Endorsement and quorum anatomy

What you are looking at. The same proposal is sent to two peers in different organisations. Each runs the chaincode, produces a read set (what it read) and a write set (what it would write), and signs the result. The client assembles the proposal from those signed responses and hands it to the orderer, which batches it into a block. Every peer then validates that block independently. The rules in the dark band are enforced in the contract body, not merely in the channel configuration — which is why the UI cannot bypass them.
Say it“Two endorsements, and the contract insists they come from two different organisations — one organisation with two officers cannot manufacture a quorum. Fabric's policy says which organisations must sign; the contract body adds the part the policy cannot express: one review per participant, and rejection is final.”

Ledger anatomy — blocks, transactions, Merkle root, world state

What you are looking at. A block header carries the previous block's hash, a Merkle root over its transaction hashes, and the ordering service's signature. The Merkle tree lets you prove that one transaction belongs to a block by supplying a sibling hash — O(log n) proof instead of handing over the whole block. Below, a transaction shows its payload hash and its two signed endorsements; on the right, world state holds the current records plus the indexes the contract maintains.
Say it“The block header holds three things that matter: the previous hash, a Merkle root over the transaction hashes, and the orderer's signature. And the ledger has two layers — the hash-linked transaction log, which is the truth, and world state, which is just a cache built from it.”

The hash chain and how tampering is localised

What you are looking at. Each block commits to the one before it, so a change anywhere cascades forward. The verifier walks the chain from genesis — not backwards from the tip — because a verifier that only checked the last link would miss an alteration further back. And it does not merely say “invalid”: it names the block, the field, and why the two no longer agree.
Say it“Hashes localise a tamper, signatures prevent one. An attacker can recompute every hash in the chain — but they still cannot produce the orderer's signature over the block header, or the endorsers' signatures over the altered transaction, without the private keys.”

One contract, three hosts

What you are looking at. The contract module has no import of Fabric, no import of the DOM, and no import of any host SDK. It receives a ChaincodeStub and does all its reading and writing through that. Each host then supplies its own implementation. This is what lets the graded demonstration open instantly in a browser while the same rules still deploy unmodified to a genuine Fabric peer.
Say it“One contract, three hosts. The business logic has zero platform imports — it takes a ChaincodeStub and nothing else. The browser supplies a MemoryStub, Cloudflare supplies a Durable Object, Fabric supplies a fabric-shim Context. The same rules run unchanged on a real peer.”

The token state machine and the end-to-end flow

What you are looking at. The status is not a label the interface chooses — it is a field in world state, and the chaincode is the only thing permitted to change it. That is why a rejected token cannot be pushed back into the review queue: the contract refuses that transition, not the interface. The invoice path mirrors the token path, with one deliberate asymmetry — settlement is granted to the funders and to the independent auditor.
Say it“The status lives in world state and only the chaincode may change it. Rejection is terminal — a rejected token can never be re-reviewed or re-approved, because the contract refuses that transition. In the interface it would just be a hidden button.”

The eight trust rules, mapped to code

What you are looking at. This is the table to memorise for the viva: eight rules, the function that enforces each one, and what happens if you try to break it. Notice that rule 8 is stronger than a Fabric private data collection — the contact number is never written at all, so there is nothing to leak, no collection to configure and no purge to schedule.
Say it“Eight rules, all in the chaincode. The proof is simple: switch to the second reviewer and try to endorse the same token — the contract refuses you, and it tells you why.”

Determinism and private data

What you are looking at. These are the two Fabric constraints that most directly shaped the code. Determinism is why the contract never calls the wall clock itself — the host supplies the block time so every endorser sees the same reading. Private data is where GreenToken deliberately chose a stronger but simpler guarantee than Fabric's, and where you should be precise rather than clever if you are asked about it.
Say it“Two Fabric constraints shaped the contract. Determinism — so the timestamp comes from the host, not from Date.now, and every endorser sees the same clock. And private data — I emulate it by stripping the contact number at the boundary, so it is never written at all. Stronger than a collection here, but not the same thing, and I would say so.”

Reads versus writes, and what the Gateway does

What you are looking at. Fabric treats reads and writes as different operations with different costs and different safety properties, and the API mirrors that split exactly. Below, the five responsibilities the v2.5 Gateway owns on the client's behalf — which is why the client SDK today makes a single SubmitTransaction() call instead of discovering and dialling peers across organisations itself.
Say it“Reads are Evaluate — they never touch the ledger and never produce a transaction. Writes are Submit, and they go through all six phases. The Gateway, standardised in Fabric v2.5, owns the five verbs so the client only makes one call.”

What is real and what is emulated

What you are looking at. This is the page that wins marks, because volunteering the boundaries is far stronger than being caught on them. The business logic and the cryptography are genuinely implemented. The network layer is simulated — and the same contract still deploys to a real Fabric peer, so the simulation is a deployment decision rather than a shortcut in the rules.
Say it“Let me be precise about the scope. The rules and the cryptography are real and running. What I simulated is the network layer — the demo host is a Durable Object — plus private data distribution, CA-issued certificates, bank settlement and on-chain governance. The identical contract deploys to a real Fabric peer with network/deploy.sh.”
