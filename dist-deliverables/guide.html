Part 1

Part 1 — Hyperledger Fabric from first principles

1.1 The problem a blockchain solves

Start from the business problem, never from the technology. If you cannot explain why a shared ledger is needed, you do not understand why any of the machinery exists.

Four parties who need to agree on one truth

Consider a carbon-credit scheme. Four groups must all believe the same thing:

  • Planters Association — plants trees and wants to be paid for what they planted.
  • State Forest Department — a government regulator that must verify plantings are real.
  • Independent Carbon Auditor — a second, separate reviewer that buyers trust.
  • Corporate Green Funders — buyers who want to buy verified carbon, cheaply, in volume.

Why the obvious solutions fail

  • A shared spreadsheet. Someone owns it. The owner can edit a row after the fact, and nobody downstream can prove it. The moment a funder disputes a planting, there is no evidence.
  • A government database run by the Forest Department. Then the Department is the only auditor of its own approvals. A buyer has no reason to believe the Department's own database.
  • Trust between the parties. Four organisations that want to transact with each other usually do not fully trust each other — that is precisely why they need a neutral record.

What a blockchain actually adds

Not speed. Not cost. Not “decentralisation” in the abstract. Three specific, testable properties:

  1. A shared state. Every participating organisation holds a copy of the same ledger, so no single party can unilaterally change what the record says.
  2. Cryptographic tamper-evidence. Each block commits to the one before it with a hash. Alter history and the break is detectable by anyone, immediately, with no trusted party needed to check.
  3. Rule enforcement by the code itself. The conditions under which a state change is valid — who may write, how many approvals are needed, what the price is — live in the smart contract, not in the goodwill of whoever writes the user interface.
The one-sentence version you should be able to say on demand

A blockchain is a ledger that several organisations share and that nobody can quietly rewrite, because every record is cryptographically linked to the one before it and the rules for changing it are enforced by code rather than by trust.

That is the whole idea. Everything else in this guide is engineering in service of it.

1.2 What a ledger is, and why a database is not enough

Part 1 — Fabric fundamentals

A ledger is an append-only record of transactions. The word append-only carries all the weight.

Ledger vs. database

  • A database is optimised for overwrite. You update a row, and the old value is gone.
  • A ledger is optimised for append. You add a new entry; nothing already written is ever modified. Current values are derived by folding history forward.

What that buys you

  • Auditability. Every state you have ever seen can be reconstructed, because nothing was thrown away.
  • Attribution. You can always answer “who asked for this, and when?”
  • Non-repudiation. A participant cannot later deny a transaction they signed.
  • Independence. Each organisation can verify the whole history for itself. Nobody has to be believed.

Why replication matters

A single copy of a ledger is just a database with extra steps. The ledger becomes meaningful when several parties hold their own copy and can compare them. If one party's copy differs, something went wrong and the difference is detectable.

What a blockchain is not

It is not a database with extra steps of scale, and it is not automatically faster, cheaper, or better. It is a specific answer to one question: how do parties who do not fully trust each other agree on a shared history without a central authority? If you have a trusted central authority, a normal database is usually the better engineering answer — and being able to say that is a sign of understanding rather than a weakness.

Read-only versus write access

This distinction matters for GreenToken and is worth stating clearly, because it is deliberate:

  • Reads are open by design. Anyone may look up any token, its location, its photo hash and its endorsements. The whole point of a carbon registry is that buyers and the public can verify what is claimed. Openness here is the feature.
  • Writes are role-gated. Only the planters association may create a token, only verifiers and auditors may endorse one, only funders may buy. The chaincode refuses anyone else.

On the demo, if you are asked “so is this private?”, the answer is: the ledger is public, the actions are permissioned. That is a deliberate design, not an oversight — and Part 2 covers what happens to genuinely private fields such as a planter’s phone number.

1.3 The hash chain — the single most important idea

Part 1 — Fabric fundamentals

If you understand one thing about Hyperledger Fabric, understand this. Every other guarantee follows from it.

A hash is a fingerprint

A cryptographic hash (SHA-256) turns any input into a fixed 256-bit fingerprint. Three properties matter:

  • Deterministic — the same input always gives the same output, on every machine, forever.
  • One-way — you cannot work backwards from the fingerprint to the input.
  • Collision-resistant — no two different inputs share a fingerprint (practically).

Chaining the blocks

Each block header stores the hash of the previous block. The hash of block N is computed over the previous hash plus the block’s own contents:

blockHash[N] = SHA256( blockHash[N-1] || merkleRoot[N] || timestamp[N] || ... )

Now change one byte anywhere in block 40. Three things happen, automatically:

  1. Block 40’s own hash changes completely.
  2. Block 41 stores block 40’s hash — so block 41’s input changed, so block 41’s hash changes.
  3. The same cascade continues to the tip of the chain.
The consequence, stated precisely

Altering history does not merely make one block wrong. It invalidates every block after it. And because the new hashes are internally consistent, an attacker could rewrite the whole chain — which is exactly why the final block is signed by the ordering service. Without that signature, recomputing the whole chain would look perfectly valid to anyone who did not check the signatures.

That is the whole tamper-evidence story: the hash chain makes alteration localise itself, and the signatures make alteration impossible to hide. Both are needed.

Merkle trees — proving one transaction without showing all of them

Each block contains a Merkle root: the transaction hashes are paired and hashed pairwise, and the tree is collapsed to a single root stored in the block header.

leaves  = [ H(tx0), H(tx1), H(tx2), H(tx3) ]
level 1 = [ H(l0+l1), H(l2+l3) ]
level 2 = [ H(l1+l2) ]        <- merkleRoot, stored in the block header

To prove that tx2 is in the block, you supply tx3 and the root, and the verifier recomputes two hashes. Proof size is O(log n), independent of how many transactions the block holds. GreenToken uses this so a single token’s membership can be verified without handing over the whole block.

What this looks like in the running app

Open Ledger Explorer and expand the top block. You are looking at exactly this: the previous hash, the Merkle root, the ordering service’s signature, and each transaction’s payload hash. Then press ⚠ Tamper with last block and watch the verifier recompute every hash from the genesis block and name the exact point where the chain stopped agreeing with itself.

1.4 The building blocks: MSP, channel, peer, orderer, chaincode

Part 1 — Fabric fundamentals

Fabric has five moving parts. Learn what each one owns, because almost every exam question about Fabric is really a question about which component is responsible for what.

MSP — Membership Service Provider

The identity directory of one organisation. It maps a participant to an X.509 certificate issued by that organisation’s certificate authority, and it defines what roles that identity may play: peer admin, peer, client, orderer.

Fabric has no user accounts and no passwords. Identity is a cryptographic certificate. A participant is a key pair.

Channel — a private ledger

A set of organisations sharing one ledger and one ordering service. It carries its own configuration transaction: which organisations belong, what endorsement policy applies, and so on. Governance is itself on-chain.

Two channels that share no organisations share no data at all. A channel is the unit of both confidentiality and governance.

Peer — the ledger holder

Owns the block store (the hash-linked history) and the state database (current values). Runs chaincode. Holds an endorsement identity and signs endorsement responses. Owned by one organisation.

A peer is not a miner and does not compete. It cooperates. Several peers per organisation give redundancy.

Orderer — the sequencer

Collects endorsed transactions, batches them into blocks in a defined order, and distributes those blocks to peers. In production it runs Raft or BFT consensus across several nodes.

The orderer does not execute chaincode and does not check business rules. It orders. Correctness comes from elsewhere.

Chaincode — the smart contract

The business logic. It runs on peers, invoked over the Fabric Gateway, and must be deterministic: every endorser that evaluates the same proposal must reach the identical result.

Fabric supports Go, Java and Node.js. This project uses Node.js/TypeScript via fabric-contract-api 2.5.

Event — the output stream

Chaincode emits an event on every committed transaction, and peers deliver those events to clients.

The Live chaincode events panel on the dashboard is exactly this — the same event stream Fabric publishes to subscribing clients.

The distinction that trips people up

The orderer sequences and the peers validate and commit. The orderer is deliberately not trusted for correctness: if it gave two peers different views of the same transaction, the endorsement policy and the validation phase would reject it. Fabric moves trust out of the sequencer and into the cryptographic protocol.

1.5 World state versus the transaction log

Part 1 — Fabric fundamentals

Fabric stores your data twice, on purpose. Understanding why explains how it is fast and auditable at the same time.

The transaction log

An append-only, hash-linked sequence of blocks. Every transaction ever committed is here, forever. This is the source of truth.

  • Written once, never modified.
  • Blocks are linked by hash.
  • Replaying it reconstructs everything.

Cost: reading current state means scanning history. This is why the log alone is not enough.

World state

A key–value database (LevelDB, or CouchDB for rich queries) holding the current value of every key.

  • Rebuilt from the log whenever a peer joins.
  • Optimised for “give me the value now”.
  • Still backed by the hash chain — it is a cache, not an authority.

Cost: you must trust the state DB is consistent with the log. Fabric guarantees it by construction, and by re-verification on demand.

The two-tier design in one line

The transaction log is the truth; world state is a derived cache. Queries hit the cache; audits read the log; and because the cache is rebuilt from the log, a corrupted cache cannot survive a rebuild.

Fabric peers gossip new blocks to each other, and they do not need to talk to the orderer for every read. This is why a peer that falls behind can catch up by replaying blocks rather than by querying a central server.

How GreenToken mirrors this

Your implementation keeps the same two layers, in the browser and in the Durable Object:

  • The block list is the transaction log — each block holds its transactions, the previous hash, the Merkle root and the signature.
  • The state map is world state — token records, invoice records and the indexes that make search queries cheap.
  • Chain verification replays the log: recompute every hash from the genesis block and confirm the state still agrees with it. That is exactly what a Fabric peer does when it rebuilds or revalidates its state.

This is why the tamper demonstration is convincing: the app does not flip a boolean. It re-derives the chain and reports where the derivation fails.

1.6 The six-phase transaction lifecycle

Part 1 — Fabric fundamentals

Every transaction that has ever run on Fabric went through these six phases. If you can narrate them, you can answer most “explain how Fabric works” questions.

1 — Proposal

The client sends a transaction proposal to a peer. The proposal contains the function to invoke and its arguments — but it is not yet a write.

2 — Endorsement

The peer runs the chaincode function, simulates what it would write, and returns a signed response containing the read set, the write set, and a signature over the response. Endorsement is collected from as many peers as the endorsement policy requires — for GreenToken’s verification step, from two different organisations.

3 — Ordering

The endorsed transactions go to the ordering service, which batches them into a block in a defined order and distributes that block to the channel’s peers. The orderer decides sequence, never meaning.

4 — Validation

Each peer independently checks the block before accepting it: were enough valid endorsements collected from the required organisations, are the signatures genuine, and is the read set still current? A transaction whose read set has gone stale is marked invalid rather than silently producing a different result.

5 — Commit

Peers that accept the block append it to their ledger and update world state. From this point the transaction is permanent.

6 — Notification

Chaincode events and commit-status events are delivered to listening clients.

The insight worth saying out loud

The chaincode was executed in phase 2, at endorsement — not in phase 5. By the time a transaction is committed, the business logic has already run, and the peers in phase 4 are only checking that the right parties agreed and that nothing changed underneath. That is exactly why Fabric chaincode must be deterministic.

1.7 Determinism, endorsement policies, private data

Part 1 — Fabric fundamentals

Determinism — why the rules are stricter than you expect

If two peers evaluating the same proposal get different answers, the network is broken. So Fabric chaincode may not:

  • read the wall clock — use the block timestamp that Fabric supplies instead;
  • generate random numbers — use a deterministic source or a seeded value;
  • call out to the network or the filesystem;
  • iterate over an unordered map without sorting the keys first.
This is a real engineering constraint, not pedantry

GreenToken’s chaincode gets its timestamps from a single nowIso() helper supplied by the host, never from Date.now() inside the contract. The host provides the block time, so every endorser sees the same clock reading.

When an examiner asks why the chaincode is written in a slightly awkward style — no direct Date.now(), no random IDs — this is the answer: determinism is a Fabric requirement, not a stylistic preference.

Endorsement policies

A policy states which parties must have endorsed a transaction for it to be valid. It can be declared at three levels:

  • Chaincode-level — the default policy for the whole contract.
  • Collection-level — for private data collections.
  • State-based — a policy attached to a particular key in world state.
AND('PlantersAssociationMSP', 'ForestDeptMSP')   both must sign
OR('ForestDeptMSP', 'AuditorMSP')                either is enough
MAJORITY                                         a majority must sign

GreenToken’s rule is stronger than a policy expression, because it is enforced in the contract body rather than in configuration: the code checks that approvals come from two distinct organisations, and separately that no single participant has already reviewed the token.

Private data — and its limits

Fabric can split a field across the organisations of a private data collection. Members see the plaintext; non-members see only the hash. But be precise about what this is:

  • It is not encryption of the whole ledger. The private value is distributed and reassembled by the authorised members.
  • The hash is on-chain and immutable. Everyone can verify that the private value was not changed, even though nobody else can read it.
  • In v2.5 you can even call PurgePrivateData() to delete the private value and its history while keeping the hash as evidence — though this needs the V2_5 channel capability.
What GreenToken does instead, and why

The planter’s contact number is stripped at the chaincode boundary before the transaction is hashed or stored, so it never reaches world state, the payload hash, or any read view. That is a simpler and stronger guarantee than a private data collection — the secret is not in the system at all. Be honest that this is an emulation of private data, not a Fabric private data collection.

1.8 Fabric versus Ethereum, and what v2.5 changed

Part 1 — Fabric fundamentals

Two questions you should be able to answer without hesitating: how is Fabric different from a public blockchain? and what does Fabric v2.5 actually give you that v2.2 did not?

DimensionHyperledger FabricEthereum
MembershipPermissioned. Identity is an X.509 certificate issued by an organisation’s MSP.Permissionless. Identity is a public key; anyone can participate.
ConsensusOrdering service (Raft / BFT) across configured nodes.Proof of Stake across thousands of validators.
Transaction finalityImmediate once committed — no probabilistic waiting.Probabilistic; finality deepens with confirmations.
ThroughputThousands of transactions per second in production designs.Limited by the consensus layer.
Fees and gasNone. No token, no gas, no miner.Gas price paid to validators in ETH.

The one-line contrast to say out loud

Fabric assumes the parties know who each other are and have already agreed to be in the same room. Ethereum assumes nobody knows anybody. That single difference explains the permissioning, the absence of fees, the throughput, and the privacy model.

Fabric is the correct choice for a consortium of a government department, an auditor and a corporate buyer. It is the wrong choice for a public token. Make that judgement out loud — it demonstrates understanding rather than enthusiasm.

What v2.5 specifically added

v2.5 is the current long-term-support release, upgradable in place from v2.2 LTS. Its headline features:

  • Fabric Gateway and its client libraries. The Gateway — introduced in v2.4 and standardised in v2.5 — is a service running on a peer that handles transaction submission for the client. It manages five things: Evaluate (query), Endorse, Submit, commit-status waiting, and chaincode events. The client application no longer has to discover and connect to peers in other organisations itself. Node, Java and Go Gateway SDKs (v1.0.0) expose a single SubmitTransaction() call.
  • Channels without a system channel. Ordering-service configuration is no longer confined to a special system channel.
  • Ledger snapshots. A new peer can join an existing channel from a snapshot instead of replaying every block from the beginning.
  • PurgePrivateData(). Delete private data and its history while keeping the immutable hash as evidence. Requires the V2_5 channel capability.

GreenToken’s chaincode targets this line: fabric-contract-api@2.5.8 and fabric-shim@2.5.8, and it is packaged and installed by network/deploy.sh against a v2.5 compose network.

Part 2 — How the GreenToken app works

2.1 The use case and the four organisations

Now that you have the Fabric model, here is your project expressed in it.

The use case

A carbon credit is only worth something if a buyer can trust that the tree exists, that it was planted where it is claimed to be, and that it has not been counted twice. Today that trust usually rests on a spreadsheet, an audit report and a photograph in an email.

GreenToken turns a planting event into a verifiable token:

  1. A planter submits the species, the number of trees, a GPS coordinate and a photo.
  2. The SHA-256 of the photo bytes and the SHA-256 of the geo payload go on the ledger. The photo itself does not.
  3. Two independent organisations review the proof and sign their endorsement with ECDSA.
  4. At quorum, the token becomes VERIFIED and can be funded.
  5. A corporate funder discovers verified tokens on a map, puts a basket together, and the contract issues one discounted invoice for the whole basket.
  6. Settlement releases the net amount to the planters, recorded on chain.
How this maps onto the two Unit 5 use cases

Use case 1 — trade / supply chain finance: a basket of verified tokens becomes a single discounted invoice that is settled to multiple planters. Provenance-priced, basket-invoiced, auto-settled.  ·  Use case 2 — government: the Forest Department, the Independent Auditor and the Planters Association share one channel, and the department’s approvals live on-chain where they can be audited.

2.2 One contract, three hosts

Part 2 — the application

This is the central architectural idea of the project, and the one most worth understanding deeply.

The problem it solves

Fabric chaincode must run inside a Fabric peer. But the graded demonstration has to open in a browser, and requiring the examiner to start a four-gigabyte Docker network before your demo begins is not a fair thing to ask. Those two requirements pull in opposite directions.

The resolution: invert the dependency

Rather than write the contract twice — once for Fabric and once for the web — write it once, with no dependency on any platform, and let each host supply the plumbing.

The contract module never imports Fabric. It receives a ChaincodeStub — a small interface with methods like getState, putState, getStateByRange, emitEvent and getCallerIdentity — and every read or write goes through it.

Why the status machine matters

The status is not a label the UI chooses — it is a field in world state, and the chaincode is the only thing that may change it. Look closely at the TreeToken type: there is no field for the planter’s phone number. That is not an oversight — the type itself is the guarantee. The contact value is stripped at the chaincode boundary before anything is hashed or stored, so it cannot leak into a read view, because it was never written in the first place.

Why the split matters

In Fabric, reads and writes are different operations with different costs and different safety properties. A read is a evaluate — it never touches the ledger and never produces a transaction. Your API exposes the same distinction: read endpoints are plain GETs; writes go through the transaction path and produce a block.

The photo hash, end to end

1. The planter picks a JPEG from their phone.
2. The app reads the bytes and computes SHA-256 over them.
3. The chaincode receives the digest, the byte count and the MIME type —
   never the image itself.
4. The digest is stored in the token record and covered by the payload hash
   of the transaction that created it.
5. The reviewer sees dec02336…6252 and can be told: re-upload that exact
   file and you get that exact digest. No two different files will do it.

The geo hash

The coordinate is hashed as well, and the hash covers the whole payload — latitude, longitude and accuracy — not just the coordinates. Changing the accuracy figure changes the hash. The reviewer sees the accuracy in metres next to the hash, because a coordinate without an accuracy is not evidence of anything.

The endorsement signature

Each endorsement is signed over a payload containing the token id, the verdict, the note, the reviewer’s organisation and the time. The signature is stored with the endorsement. Two consequences matter for the demo:

  • The reviewer cannot later claim they approved something else, or that the note was different.
  • The contract can verify that the signature belongs to the organisation it claims to.

The block signature and Merkle root

Each block carries the ordering service’s signature over its header, and a Merkle root over its transaction hashes. Together they mean: a peer that receives a block can check that it came from the ordering service, and that its transactions are intact.

One simplification, stated plainly

The public keys used for the endorsements are derived deterministically from the participant id rather than being issued by a certificate authority. A real Fabric deployment would use X.509 certificates from each organisation’s MSP. The signatures are real ECDSA signatures; the key distribution is the part that is simplified.

2.6 The eight trust rules

Part 2 — the application

These are the rules that make the project interesting. Every one of them lives in the chaincode, not in the interface.

01

Quorum needs two different organisations

Two approvals, but from two distinct MSPs. One organisation with two officers cannot manufacture quorum. Two separate guards enforce it: one review per participant, and one review per organisation.

02

Rejection is terminal

A rejected token can never be re-reviewed, re-approved, or pushed back into the queue. A rejection is evidence, not a speed bump.

03

One photo cannot mint two tokens

A duplicate photo hash is rejected. This blocks the “photograph the same tree twice and sell both credits” attack, which is the oldest carbon-credit fraud there is.

04

The price is on-chain

BASE_PRICE_INR = 150 per verified tree. The client cannot negotiate it and the funder cannot dictate it.

05

The discount ladder is in the contract

500 units → 15%, 200 → 10%, 50 → 5%, 1 → 0%. The invoice computes it. If the browser sent a 40% discount, the contract would ignore it and use the ladder.

06

Only VERIFIED tokens can be funded

The contract validates the whole basket. If a single token is not VERIFIED, the entire invoice is refused — it does not quietly drop the ineligible one.

07

Private contact never reaches the ledger

Stripped at the chaincode boundary before hashing, storing or emitting. The TreeToken type has no field for it.

08

GPS accuracy is recorded, and shown to the reviewer

The reviewer sees the accuracy radius before approving — the seeded example includes a rejection at 320 metres. Accuracy is not yet a hard threshold; that is listed as future scope.

How to demonstrate that these are real

Pick any one of these rules and ask: “what happens if I try to break it?” Then open the Verification page as the second reviewer and try to endorse the same token. The contract stops you, and it stops you in a message that says why. The rule is not in the interface — the interface is only showing you what the contract decided.

3.3 The tamper moment, in detail

Part 3 — the demo

This is ninety seconds, and it is the reason the project exists. Here is exactly what happens, so you can narrate it accurately rather than gesturing at a red card.

What the button actually does

  1. It reaches into the last committed block and alters a transaction’s stored payload — changing a value that was already signed.
  2. It leaves the block’s recorded hash untouched, which is the whole point: the block now contains a payload that does not match its own hash.

What the verifier does next

  1. It walks the chain from the genesis block forward, not from the tip backwards. This matters — a verifier that only checks the last link would miss an alteration further back.
  2. For each block it recomputes the hash from the previous hash, the Merkle root and the timestamp.
  3. It recomputes each transaction’s payload hash and compares it with the signed value.
  4. It recomputes the Merkle root from the leaves.
  5. At the first block where a recomputed value disagrees with the stored one, it reports the break: which block, what field, and why the two no longer agree.
Why it says “block 32” and not just “invalid”

A verdict without a location is not evidence — it is an assertion. Naming the block and the field is what makes this a demonstration rather than a claim. Say the location out loud when you show it; that is the difference between showing a feature and proving a property.

Why the restore works

Restoring does not patch the broken block. It rebuilds the chain from the stored transactions, recomputing every hash and re-signing every block, then writes the result back. The chain after a restore is not the chain before — it is an independently reconstructed chain that happens to be equivalent. If any stored transaction had been genuinely lost, the restore could not reproduce it.

The three things to say about it

  • “I altered a signed payload.” Not “I changed a value in a database”. The payload was covered by a signature and a hash.
  • “The verifier found it, and named where.” Location is the substance of the claim.
  • “It is repeatable.” The examiner can click the same button. That is the difference between a video of a tamper test and a tamper test.

If someone asks “could an attacker just recompute everything?”

They could recompute the hashes — that is the easy half. They could not produce the ordering service’s signature over the new block headers, nor the endorsers’ signatures over the altered transactions, without the corresponding private keys. That is why the signature is stored alongside the hash and why the verifier checks both.

Part 4

Part 4 — Viva preparation

4.1 Question bank, with answers

Twenty questions an examiner is likely to ask, with answers that are accurate to this codebase. Read the answers aloud once — a rehearsed answer sounds like knowledge.

1. What exactly is a blockchain?
A ledger that several organisations share and that nobody can quietly rewrite, because every record is cryptographically linked to the one before it and the rules for changing it are enforced by code rather than by trust.
2. Why is a hash chain enough? Why not just encrypt things?
Encryption protects data from people who should not read it. A hash chain proves that nobody changed what everyone can already read. GreenToken needs both — the ledger is deliberately public, so the guarantee has to be tamper-evidence, not confidentiality.
3. Is this real Hyperledger Fabric, or a simulation?
The smart contract is real Fabric v2.5 TypeScript chaincode, in chaincode/src/contract.ts, deployable with network/deploy.sh. The business logic has no platform imports, so each host supplies its own stub. The demo ran on a Cloudflare Durable Object — a real ordering-and-commit host that needs no Docker. The same contract runs unchanged on Fabric.
4. Why did you deploy to Cloudflare rather than only shipping Fabric?
Because a Fabric network is about four gigabytes of Docker images and a multi-step install. A demo that fails to start scores zero. Shipping both means the demonstration always works, and the Fabric deployment is still real, documented and repeatable in the lab.
5. Where is the data stored?
Two layers, as in Fabric itself. The transaction log is the append-only hash-linked block history — the source of truth. World state is a key–value cache of current values, rebuilt from the log. The app verifies by replaying the log.
6. Can a planter fake it?
They would have to photograph the same tree twice, and each photo’s SHA-256 is bound to a geo-hash and a distinct token id, so a duplicate photo hash is rejected outright. The reviewer sees the GPS accuracy. And they still need two endorsements from organisations the planter does not control.
7. Why two endorsements? Isn't one enough?
One approver is the original trust problem — the approver becomes the thing you have to trust. Two independent organisations, with the contract enforcing that no single participant and no single organisation may review the same token twice. The rule lives in the chaincode, not in the UI.
8. What if the Forest Department is corrupt?
That is exactly why the second signature comes from a separate organisation, and why auditors can read everything while planters’ contact details never enter the ledger. Full on-chain governance, where the consortium changes the rules by vote, is listed as future scope.
9. How do I know the dashboard numbers are real?
They are read out of ledger state by the same read models the HTTP API exposes. I can show you the endpoint. Nothing on that dashboard is typed in by hand — and the leaderboard is a query over ledger state rather than a stored score.
10. What is a Merkle root and why do you need it?
The transaction hashes are paired and hashed pairwise up to a single root, which is stored in the block header. It proves that a given transaction is in a given block without handing over the whole block — proof size is logarithmic in the number of transactions.
11. Why does the contract avoid Date.now()?
Fabric chaincode has to be deterministic: every endorser evaluating the same proposal must reach the identical result. Wall-clock time, randomness and external calls are all forbidden. The contract takes its timestamp from the host, which supplies the block time, so every endorser sees the same clock reading.
12. What is an endorsement policy?
A rule saying which parties must have signed for a transaction to be valid — for example AND('PlantersAssociationMSP','ForestDeptMSP'). GreenToken’s rule is stronger than a policy expression, because the contract body itself checks that the approvals come from two distinct organisations.
13. How does the private data work?
In GreenToken the planter’s contact is stripped at the chaincode boundary before it is hashed, stored or emitted — so it never reaches world state at all. Fabric also has private data collections, which distribute a value to authorised members while everyone sees only the hash; I chose the stronger, simpler guarantee here and label it as an emulation.
14. How does the discount actually get applied?
The ladder lives in the contract — 500 units to 15%, 200 to 10%, 50 to 5%, 1 to 0%. FundTokens computes it from the basket size and writes gross, discount and net into the invoice. The client cannot choose its own discount.
15. What happens on a rejected token?
Nothing can happen. Rejection is terminal: the contract refuses any further review, approval or re-inversion back into the queue. A rejection is evidence, not a speed bump.
16. Is the price negotiable?
No. BASE_PRICE_INR = 150 per verified tree is fixed in the contract. The client cannot negotiate it and the funder cannot dictate it. The discount is the only lever, and it is also in the contract.
17. What is the biggest limitation?
The demo host is a Durable Object, not a Fabric peer — the network layer is simulated even though the rules and cryptography are not. Settlement also marks an invoice settled without moving money. Both are stated plainly in the README and on the limitations slide of the deck.
18. How would you scale this to thousands of planters?
The channel and the contract are already the right shape: peers per organisation, indexes by district and species, and read models served off state. The next real step is replacing the Durable Object host with a Fabric network, because the contract does not change — only the stub does.
19. Why can reads be public?
Deliberately. This is a visibility use case: the point of a carbon registry is that buyers and the public can verify what is claimed. Only writes are role-gated, and the chaincode refuses any write from a role that does not hold it.
20. What would you do next?
On-chain governance so the consortium can change the discount ladder by vote; a Fabric private data collection for the planter’s contact; a real oracle for carbon pricing; and a pager-and-email threshold for GPS accuracy so a poor fix is rejected automatically rather than shown to the reviewer.

A closing note on how to present all of this

You built something with a genuine hash chain, real signatures, real consensus rules, and a deployment that runs on the public internet. Present it as what it is: a working system with a clearly stated scope. Specificity — about both the strengths and the limitations — is what makes the rest of it credible.
